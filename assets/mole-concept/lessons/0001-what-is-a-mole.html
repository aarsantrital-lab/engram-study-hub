🔢 Lesson 1 — What Is A Mole?

1 of 1 · ~8 min read · for exam survival

The problem

Atoms are absurdly small. A single carbon atom weighs about 1.99 × 10−23 g. You can't weigh one atom on a lab balance. You can't count individual atoms either.

Chemists needed a bridge — a way to go from "number of atoms" to "grams on a scale."

The solution: the mole

A mole is a counting unit — just like:

  • 1 dozen = 12 eggs
  • 1 ream = 500 sheets of paper
  • 1 mole = 6.022 × 1023 particles

That number — 6.022 × 1023 — is called Avogadro's number (NA).

Why 6.022 × 1023?
Because 1 mole of carbon-12 atoms weighs exactly 12 g. Avogadro's number is how many atoms that takes.

Molar mass (M)

The molar mass is the mass of 1 mole of a substance, in grams per mole (g/mol).

On the periodic table, the atomic mass number is the molar mass in g/mol:

ElementAtomic massMolar mass
Carbon (C)1212 g/mol
Oxygen (O)1616 g/mol
Hydrogen (H)11 g/mol
Sodium (Na)2323 g/mol

For molecules, add them up:

H₂O: H = 2 × 1 = 2, O = 16 → M = 18 g/mol
CO₂: C = 12, O = 2 × 16 = 32 → M = 44 g/mol
NaCl: Na = 23, Cl = 35.5 → M = 58.5 g/mol

The golden formula

n = m ÷ M
n= number of moles (mol)
m= mass of the sample (g)
M= molar mass (g/mol)
Also useful:
Number of particles = n × NA = n × 6.022 × 1023

Worked example

Q: How many moles are in 36 g of water (H₂O)?

Step 1: Find molar mass of H₂O
  H = 2 × 1 = 2, O = 16 → M = 18 g/mol

Step 2: Apply n = m ÷ M
  n = 36 ÷ 18 = 2 mol

Bonus: How many molecules?
  2 × 6.022 × 1023 = 1.204 × 1024 molecules
