    What Is a Mole?

    The big idea

    A mole is a counting word. It counts 6.022 × 10²³ particles — nothing else.

    A shopkeeper says one dozen eggs and means 12 eggs. He says one ream of paper and means 500 sheets. Neither word says anything about mass: a dozen eggs and a dozen bricks are both twelve things. Mole is the same kind of word, with a much bigger number attached — one mole is 6.022 × 10²³ particles of whatever you are counting. That number has a name, the Avogadro constant, written NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

    n = m ÷ M   ·   N = n × NA

    Nobody can count particles directly, so chemistry weighs them instead. The molar mass M, in g/mol, is the mass of one mole of a substance, and it is the only bridge between the two worlds: grams on the balance on one side, particles that react on the other. Every stoichiometry question in the NEB paper crosses that bridge at least once, which is why this one lesson sits underneath about a third of the chemistry marks you are currently losing.

    The method

    The three-box road — mass ⇄ moles ⇄ particles

    Three boxes, two arrows. Grams is what a balance reads. Moles is the middle box and every question passes through it. Particles is a bare count with no unit. You can never jump from grams straight to particles; the road goes through the middle box both ways.

    The atomic masses used in this whole topic — memorise this board before any molar-mass work, because the paper does not always print it:

    ElementHCN ONaS ClCa
    Atomic mass (amu, per atom)1121416233235.540
    Molar mass (g/mol, per mole)1121416233235.540

    Same numbers, two different units. One carbon atom has a mass of 12 amu; one mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12 g, so its molar mass is 12 g/mol. Writing amu where g/mol belongs is a marked error even when the digits are right.

    Step 1 · Name the boxes
    Write what you are given and what is asked, each with its unit. g means the grams box, mol means the middle box, "how many molecules" means the particle box. Two seconds here decides the whole road.
    Step 2 · Build M
    Add the atomic masses of every atom in the formula, in g/mol. For H2O: 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/mol. For CO2: 12 + 2(16) = 44 g/mol. Count the subscript on every element, hydrogen included.
    Step 3 · Cross to moles
    n = m ÷ M, that is g ÷ g/mol = mol. The units cancel to mol, which is the check that the division went the right way round. Going back is m = n × M.
    Step 4 · Cross to particles
    N = n × NA, that is mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = a bare count. Coming back from particles to moles is a division by 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

    Write the unit on every line. An examiner marks the line, not your intention.

    Watch the bridge carry mass into a count

    Predict first. Keep water on the balance (M = 18 g/mol) and change the sample from 18 g to 36 g. What happens to the number of water molecules in the sample?

    Now act

    Why it behaved that way

    Two things move the number of dots, and they move it in opposite directions. More grams on the balance means more particles, so n rises with m. A heavier molar mass means each mole costs more grams, so the same mass buys fewer moles and n falls as M rises. Set m = 18 g with M = 18 g/mol and you get exactly 1 mol; set m = 18 g with M = 36 g/mol and you get 0.5 mol from the same reading on the balance. The balance never changed — the substance did.

    That is Step 3 of the three-box road doing its work: grams alone say nothing about how many particles are present until M is known.

    Worked example · rung 1 of 4 (fully worked)

    88 g of carbon dioxide, all the way to molecules

    A cylinder is emptied of 88 g of CO2. Find the amount in mol and the number of molecules. Atomic masses: C = 12, O = 16 (g/mol).

    ElementAtoms in CO₂Atomic mass (g/mol) Mass contributed (g/mol)Running total (g/mol)
    C1121212
    O2163244
    M(CO₂)———44
    n = 88 g ÷ 44 g/mol = 2 molN = 2 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.2044 × 10²⁴ molecules

    Units check: g ÷ g/mol leaves mol, and mol × mol⁻¹ leaves a bare number. A count of molecules carries no unit — writing "1.2044 × 10²⁴ g" there loses the mark even though the digits are right.

    Self-explanation (do not skip): say out loud why the same 88 g of oxygen gas, O2 (M = 32 g/mol), would not give 2 mol. If the sentence does not come, read Step 3 again — that sentence is the lesson.

    Retrieval check 1 of 2 · notes closed

    Write the three-box road from memory: the three boxes, the arrow between each pair, and the unit that comes out.

    Model answer. Box 1 mass in g → divide by M in g/mol → Box 2 amount in mol → multiply by NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ → Box 3 number of particles, a bare count. Reverse the road by reversing both operations: divide by NA, then multiply by M.

    Two marks live here in a 5-mark question: one for n = m ÷ M with the unit, one for N = n × NA. An answer that lands on the right number with no visible road scores the answer mark only.

    Where marks are lost

    Pitfalls

    "A mole is a kind of mass — one mole of anything weighs the same." CH-M01
    A mole is an amount of substance, measured in mol, and it counts particles. One mole of water has a mass of 18 g, one mole of sodium chloride 58.5 g, one mole of calcium carbonate 100 g. The count is identical; the mass is not. Mass is the thing the balance reads, and it always needs its own symbol, m in g.
    Why it feels right: every mole calculation you meet begins with a mass in grams and the two numbers sit next to each other on the page, so the words start to feel interchangeable long before anyone says out loud that mol and g are different quantities.
    "Equal masses hold equal numbers of particles." CH-M02
    Take 44 g of CO2 (M = 44 g/mol) and 44 g of water (M = 18 g/mol). The first is 1 mol, so 6.022 × 10²³ molecules; the second is 44 ÷ 18 = 2.44 mol, so 1.47 × 10²⁴ molecules — well over twice as many, from the same reading on the balance. Equal masses hold equal numbers of particles only when the molar masses are equal.
    Why it feels right: in everyday life equal weights of the same kind of thing really do mean equal counts — two kilograms of rice is twice one kilogram. The rule only breaks when the two samples are different substances, which is exactly the case every exam question picks.
    "Molecular mass in amu and molar mass in g/mol are different quantities." CH-M03
    They are the same number wearing different units. One molecule of water has a mass of 18 amu; one mole of water — that is 6.022 × 10²³ molecules — has a mass of 18 g, so M(H2O) = 18 g/mol. In working, always write g/mol, because n = m ÷ M only cancels to mol when M carries g/mol.
    Why it feels right: two different unit names look like two different physical quantities, and the sentence "same number, different unit" is rarely said out loud in class even once.
    "6.022 × 10²³ is a mass, or a volume, or grams per mole." CH-M08
    NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ is a count per mole and nothing else. It never appears with g attached. Multiplying a mass in g by NA produces a number with the unit g/mol, which is not a count of anything — that unit on the line is the signal that the road was walked from the wrong box.
    Why it feels right: it is the only large number on the page and every other large number in chemistry so far has been a mass, so the hand reaches for it whenever an answer looks too small.

    Rung 2 · Completion — the start is done for you

    Find the amount in mol, and the number of molecules, in 9.8 g of sulphuric acid, H₂SO₄. (H = 1, S = 32, O = 16 g/mol.)

    M(H2SO4) = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98 g/mol

    Two lines are left: cross to moles, then cross to particles.

    1. 1n = m ÷ M = 9.8 g ÷ 98 g/mol = 0.1 molg ÷ g/mol cancels to mol — that unit is half the mark.
    2. 1N = n × NA = 0.1 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹Particles always come from the middle box, never straight from grams.
    3. 1N = 6.022 × 10²² molecules0.1 of 10²³ is 10²², so the power drops by one. A bare count, no unit.

    Rung 3 · Faded — one nudge, then it is yours

    What mass of calcium carbonate, CaCO₃, is 0.25 mol? (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Nudge: this one walks the road backwards, from the middle box to grams.

    1. 1M(CaCO3) = 40 + 12 + 3(16) g/molThe subscript 3 sits on the oxygen only, so 3 × 16 = 48.
    2. 1M(CaCO3) = 100 g/mol40 + 12 + 48 = 100. Worth writing on its own line — it is a mark.
    3. 1Rearrange n = m ÷ M to m = n × MGoing from the middle box to grams reverses the arrow.
    4. 1m = 0.25 mol × 100 g/mol = 25 gmol × g/mol cancels to g, which is what the question asked for.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 4 · Cold — exam conditions, no help on the page

    A sample of sodium sulphate, Na₂SO₄, contains 3.011 × 10²³ formula units. Find (a) the amount in mol and (b) the mass of the sample in g. (Na = 23, S = 32, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Worth 5 marks in the real paper. Give yourself 12 minutes — the exam pays about 2.4 minutes per mark.

    1. 1n = N ÷ NA = 3.011 × 10²³ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹Coming from the particle box means dividing, never multiplying.
    2. 1n = 0.5 mol3.011 is exactly half of 6.022, and the powers of ten cancel, so only the halving is left.
    3. 1M(Na2SO4) = 2(23) + 32 + 4(16) g/molThe subscript 2 belongs to sodium alone and the 4 to oxygen alone.
    4. 1M(Na2SO4) = 46 + 32 + 64 = 142 g/molIts own line, its own mark.
    5. 1m = n × M = 0.5 mol × 142 g/mol = 71 gmol × g/mol = g. The final answer carries its unit.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Now the pretest

    What you predicted before the lesson

    Delayed on purpose. Comparing what you thought then with what you know now is the part that sticks — especially anywhere you were confident and wrong.

    P1 · Because a weighable sample holds a huge number of particles, and reactions count particles.
    Reactions happen particle by particle, and 18 g of water — one spoonful — already holds 6.022 × 10²³ molecules. The mole is the counting word that turns a reading on the balance into that number.

    P2 · The number of particles sitting in each beaker.
    1 mol is 6.022 × 10²³ particles of anything, so both beakers hold the same count. Their masses differ: 18 g against 58.5 g. Equal amount in mol, unequal mass in g — that pair of sentences is misconception CH-M02 disarmed.

    P3 · The number was chosen so that the periodic-table number becomes grams per mole.
    One mole is defined as the number of particles that makes the mass in grams match the atomic mass number: 12 g of carbon holds exactly 6.022 × 10²³ atoms. Had the number been 1000, the mole would still be a valid count, but the molar mass of carbon would be an unusable 1.99 × 10⁻²⁰ g/mol and the periodic table would help nobody. Compare that with what you wrote before the lesson.
