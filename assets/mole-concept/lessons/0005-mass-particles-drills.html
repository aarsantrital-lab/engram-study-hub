    Mass, Moles and Particles — the Multi-Step Drill

    About 25 minutes · PACER P (procedural) · In the NEB paper this is the shape of the Group A mole question and of the two-part Group B calculation that starts "Calculate the number of…"

    Before any teaching

    Pretest — 3 questions

    The big idea

    There is one road. Exam questions only change where you get on and where you get off.

    Four lessons have built four arrows, and every mole question in the paper is a walk along them: grams to moles, moles to particles, moles across a balanced equation, and each of those in reverse. A question that looks new is nearly always the same road entered at a different point — and a two-part question is the road walked twice.

    g ⇄ mol ⇄ particles  ·  mol ⇄ mol (equation ratio)  ·  mol → L (gases at STP only)

    The marks here are not lost on the chemistry. They are lost on getting on at the wrong place, on multiplying where a division was needed, and on a final line with no unit. So this lesson drills direction and finishes every answer with the same four-item checklist.

    The method

    The road map, then the answer checklist

    Before any arithmetic, spend ten seconds drawing the road. It costs nothing in the exam and it is the difference between a multiplication and a division.

    The atomic masses this topic uses — read the board before any molar-mass work, because the paper does not always print it:

    Step 1 · Mark both ends
    Underline what the question gives, with its unit, and circle what it asks for. g, mol, "how many molecules", "how many atoms" and L are five different destinations, and the last two are where most of the lost marks live.
    Step 2 · Draw the arrows
    Sketch the road between those two ends before calculating. Particles to grams is two arrows, never one. Grams of one substance to grams of another is four.
    Step 3 · Walk one arrow per line
    One arrow, one line, unit written. Combining two arrows on one line is how a division becomes a multiplication, and the examiner cannot award a method mark for a line that is not there.
    Step 4 · Run the checklist
    Unit on the final line · significant figures matching the data · does the size make sense · did the question ask for molecules or for atoms. Four seconds, and it is the cheapest mark in the paper.

    Write the unit on every line. An examiner marks the line, not your intention.

    Interactive · predict first

    Same count of particles, different reading on the balance

    This model walks the road backwards, which is the direction the exam prefers. You set the number of particles and the molar mass; it reports the amount and the mass. Each dot is 0.1 mol. Commit to a prediction first.

    Predict first. Two samples each hold 3.011 × 10²³ particles. Sample A has M = 40 g/mol and sample B has M = 80 g/mol. How does B's mass compare with A's?

    Now act

    Why it behaved that way

    Coming from the particle end, the first arrow is always a division by 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ and the second is always a multiplication by M. Notice what the molar mass slider does and does not do: it never changes the number of dots, because the count of particles was fixed by the other slider — it only changes what those particles weigh. That is the whole of misconception CH-M02 in one screen: how many, and how heavy, are two independent questions joined only by M.

    That is Step 2 of the road map. Drawing the two arrows first is what stops the division from turning into a multiplication.

    Worked example · rung 1 of 4 (fully worked)

    The mass of 9.033 × 10²³ molecules of carbon dioxide

    Find the mass of 9.033 × 10²³ molecules of CO2, and the number of oxygen atoms in that sample. (C = 12, O = 16 g/mol.)

    ArrowWhat you haveOperationResultUnit
    particles → n9.033 × 10²³ molecules÷ 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹1.5mol
    build MC + 2 O12 + 2(16)44g/mol
    n → mass1.5 mol× 44 g/mol66g
    atoms inside1.5 mol of CO₂× 2 O per molecule × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹1.807 × 10²⁴O atoms
    Answer66 g of CO₂, containing 1.807 × 10²⁴ oxygen atoms

    Units check: a count ÷ mol⁻¹ leaves mol, and mol × g/mol leaves g. The oxygen line multiplies by 2 before the Avogadro constant, because there are 2 oxygen atoms in every molecule — 3 mol of oxygen atoms in all.

    Self-explanation (do not skip): say out loud why the answer to the second part is bigger than 6.022 × 10²³ even though the sample is less than 2 mol. If the sentence does not come, read the atoms line again — that sentence is the lesson.

    Retrieval check 1 of 2 · notes closed

    Write the four arrows with the operation each one carries, then the four items of the answer checklist.

    Model answer. Arrows: mass ÷ M gives mol · mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ gives a count · mol × coefficient ratio gives mol of another substance · mol × 22.4 L/mol gives litres, for gases at STP only. Reverse any arrow by swapping × and ÷. Checklist: unit on the final line · significant figures matching the data · is the size sensible · molecules or atoms.

    The checklist is worth about a mark a paper on its own — a right number with no unit is marked as an incomplete answer.

    Where marks are lost

    Pitfalls

    "Multiply by 6.022 × 10⁻²³ to go back to moles." CH-M08
    The sign of the power flips only if you invert the constant exactly, and 1 ÷ 6.022 is 0.166, not 6.022. Going from particles to moles is a division by 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, written as a division. The answer catches it too: 1.204 × 10²⁴ particles is 2 mol, and the wrong route gives about 72.5 — a number with no unit that belongs to nothing.
    Why it feels right: multiplying by a negative power is the standard way to undo a large multiplication in maths class, and it works whenever the mantissa is 1. Here the mantissa is 6.022, and that is exactly where it breaks.
    "One mole of water is 6.022 × 10²³ atoms." CH-M04
    It is 6.022 × 10²³ molecules, and each of those holds 3 atoms — 2 hydrogen and 1 oxygen — so the sample holds 3 mol of atoms, that is 1.807 × 10²⁴ atoms. Read the question's noun before answering: molecules, atoms, ions and formula units are four different counts from the same sample, and examiners choose the noun deliberately.
    Why it feels right: "particles" was used loosely all through Lesson 1 to mean whatever the substance is made of, and the distinction only starts to matter when a compound rather than an element is being counted.
    "Divide by 22.4 — every mole question has a 22.4 in it somewhere." CH-M06
    22.4 L/mol is the volume of one mole of an ideal gas at STP, which is 273 K and 1 atm. It says nothing about a solid, nothing about a liquid, and nothing about a gas at room temperature. Applying it to 50 g of calcium carbonate, a solid, produces a volume that is meaningless. Use it only when the question says gas and says STP or normal temperature and pressure.
    Why it feels right: 22.4 is the one memorable number in the chapter after 6.022 × 10²³, and under exam pressure a remembered number feels like a needed number.
    "The heavier sample must contain more particles." CH-M02
    1 g of hydrogen gas is 0.5 mol; 1 g of oxygen gas is 0.031 mol. The same reading on the balance, sixteen times the number of molecules, because M(H2) = 2 g/mol against M(O2) = 32 g/mol. Mass and count are joined only by M, so a comparison of counts always goes through n = m ÷ M first.
    Why it feels right: for two samples of the same substance it is perfectly true, and that is the case met most often in class. Exam questions compare different substances precisely because the intuition fails there.

    Molecules, or atoms — read the noun

    How many oxygen atoms are there in 0.5 mol of carbon dioxide, CO2?

    Now use it: without writing any working, say how many carbon atoms are in the same sample. (3.011 × 10²³.)

    Three problems, scaffolding removed one rung at a time

    Rung 1 (fully worked) was the table above. These are rungs 2, 3 and 4.

    Rung 2 · Completion — the start is done for you

    A cylinder holds 1.5 mol of nitrogen gas, N₂. Find (a) its mass and (b) the number of nitrogen atoms it contains. (N = 14 g/mol.)

    M(N2) = 2(14) = 28 g/mol

    Three lines are left: the mass, the amount of atoms, then the count.

    1. 1m = n × M = 1.5 mol × 28 g/mol = 42 gPart (a). mol × g/mol leaves g.
    2. 1n(N atoms) = 1.5 mol × 2 = 3.0 molTwo atoms in every N₂ molecule, so the amount of atoms is doubled first.
    3. 1N = 3.0 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.807 × 10²⁴ atomsPart (b). A bare count, no unit attached.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 3 · Faded — one nudge, then it is yours

    How many hydrogen atoms are there in 3.6 g of water, H₂O? (H = 1, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Nudge: three arrows, in this order — grams, moles of molecules, moles of atoms.

    1. 1M(H₂O) = 2(1) + 16 = 18 g/molIts own line, its own mark.
    2. 1n(H₂O) = 3.6 g ÷ 18 g/mol = 0.20 molGrams leave the mass world before anything else happens.
    3. 1n(H atoms) = 0.20 mol × 2 = 0.40 molTwo hydrogen atoms per molecule of water.
    4. 1N = 0.40 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 2.409 × 10²³ atomsCheck the size: less than one mole of atoms, so under 6.022 × 10²³. It is.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 4 · Cold — exam conditions, no help on the page

    A 5.85 g sample of sodium chloride, NaCl, is weighed out. Find (a) the amount in mol, (b) the number of formula units in the sample, and (c) the mass of sodium sulphate, Na₂SO₄, that would contain the same number of sodium ions. (Na = 23, Cl = 35.5, S = 32, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Worth 5 marks in the real paper. Give yourself 12 minutes — the exam pays about 2.4 minutes per mark.

    1. 1M(NaCl) = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol; n = 5.85 g ÷ 58.5 g/mol = 0.10 molPart (a). g ÷ g/mol leaves mol.
    2. 1N = 0.10 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 6.022 × 10²² formula unitsPart (b). One tenth of 10²³ is 10²², so the power drops by one.
    3. 1n(Na⁺) = 0.10 mol, since NaCl holds one sodium ion per formula unitStating the ion ratio is where part (c) is won or lost.
    4. 1Na₂SO₄ holds 2 sodium ions each, so n(Na₂SO₄) = 0.10 ÷ 2 = 0.05 molFewer formula units are needed because each one carries two sodium ions.
    5. 1M(Na₂SO₄) = 142 g/mol; m = 0.05 mol × 142 g/mol = 7.1 gPart (c). Final answer with its unit.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Now the pretest

    What you predicted before the lesson

    Delayed on purpose. Comparing what you thought then with what you know now is the part that sticks — especially anywhere you were confident and wrong.

    P1 · Hydrogen, about sixteen times as many molecules.
    n = 1 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 0.5 mol of H₂ against n = 1 g ÷ 32 g/mol = 0.031 mol of O₂. The balance reads the same in both hands; the counts differ by the ratio of the molar masses, which is 16.

    P2 · Three times as many, one for each atom in CO₂.
    1 mol of CO₂ is 6.022 × 10²³ molecules, and each molecule holds 1 carbon and 2 oxygen atoms, so the sample holds 3 mol of atoms — 1.807 × 10²⁴ of them. Molecules and atoms are different nouns and different counts.

    P3 · He multiplied where the road requires a division, and the answer has no unit it could belong to.
    The correct line is n = 1.204 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 2 mol. His route gives about 72.5, which is neither a sensible amount for that many particles nor a number carrying mol. Size checks catch this error faster than re-reading the working. Compare that with what you wrote before the lesson.
