    Mole Ratios from a Balanced Equation

    About 22 minutes · PACER P (procedural) · In the NEB paper this shows up as a 5-mark Group B calculation and as a step inside most 8-mark Group C answers on gas laws, redox and industrial manufacture

    The big idea

    A balanced equation is a recipe in particles. Grams are never allowed into it.

    The big numbers in front of the formulas — the coefficients — count particles. Multiply every one of them by 6.022 × 10²³ and they count moles, because that is what a mole is. They have never counted grams, and pouring a mass straight into the ratio is the single most common error in this unit; it appears in students' answers and occasionally on the board.

    mass (g) → n = m ÷ M → × mole ratio → m = n × M → mass (g)

    Read the equation aloud as particles before touching a calculator. For 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O:

    ReadingH₂O₂H₂O
    Molecules (the picture)212
    Dozens of molecules212
    Moles (×6.022 × 10²³)212
    Mass (g), using M43236

    Three rows carry the same three numbers; the mass row does not, and cannot, because a hydrogen molecule and an oxygen molecule have nothing like the same mass. Everything in this lesson exists to keep the calculation on the first three rows until the very last step.

    The method

    The detour — never travel from grams to grams directly

    Grams cannot talk to grams. The road leaves the mass world, crosses in moles, and comes back. Four lines, and the middle two are where the marks sit.

    The atomic masses this topic uses — read the board before any molar-mass work, because the paper does not always print it:

    ElementHCN ONaS ClCa
    Atomic mass (amu, per atom)1121416233235.540
    Molar mass (g/mol, per mole)1121416233235.540
    Step 1 · Balance, then say it in particles
    Check the equation balances, and read the coefficients aloud as molecules. Balancing is done by changing coefficients only — the subscripts inside a formula are part of the substance's identity and changing one writes a different substance.
    Step 2 · Leave the mass world
    n = m ÷ M for the substance you were given. This is Lesson 1's arrow. Until this line exists, the mole ratio has nothing legal to act on.
    Step 3 · Cross with the mole ratio
    n(wanted) = n(given) × coefficient of wanted ÷ coefficient of given. Write the ratio as a fraction with the wanted substance on top, so the given substance cancels. Both sides of this line are in mol.
    Step 4 · Come back to the balance
    m = n × M for the substance you were asked about. Its own molar mass, not the one from Step 2 — that swap is a favourite of examiners setting distractors.

    Write the unit on every line. An examiner marks the line, not your intention.

    Interactive · predict first · particles before arithmetic

    Count the molecules before you weigh anything

    The reaction is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. You choose how many molecules of each gas go into the flask; each dot below is one molecule of water that comes out. No grams appear anywhere in this model. Commit to a prediction first.

    Predict first. Six molecules of H2 and six molecules of O2 are put into a flask and the reaction goes to completion. How many molecules of water come out?

    Now act

    Why it behaved that way

    The flask does not care what anything weighs. It pairs 2 molecules of hydrogen with 1 molecule of oxygen, again and again, until one of the two runs out; whatever is left of the other one stays in the flask untouched and is called the excess. Multiply every molecule count on the screen by 6.022 × 10²³ and the same picture is now in moles — the coefficients are true at both scales, which is exactly why a chemist can weigh grams in the lab and still reason about particles.

    That is Step 1 and Step 3 of the detour. Steps 2 and 4 exist only to translate the picture into and out of the balance reading.

    Worked example · rung 1 of 4 (fully worked)

    28 g of nitrogen, with hydrogen in excess

    In the Haber process, N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3. What mass of ammonia is produced from 28 g of nitrogen when hydrogen is in excess? (N = 14, H = 1 g/mol.) "In excess" means there is more than enough hydrogen, so nitrogen alone decides the answer.

    SubstanceCoefficient (particles, mol)M (g/mol) n (mol)m (g)
    N₂ (given)1281.0028
    H₂ (excess)323.006
    NH₃ (wanted)2172.0034
    n(NH₃) = 1.00 mol × 2 ÷ 1 = 2.00 molm(NH₃) = 2.00 mol × 17 g/mol = 34 g

    Units check: g ÷ g/mol = mol on the way in, mol × g/mol = g on the way out, and the ratio line is mol ÷ mol so it changes no unit. Mass check: 28 g + 6 g = 34 g, which is the ammonia produced — nothing was created or lost.

    Self-explanation (do not skip): say out loud why the answer is 34 g and not 56 g, when the coefficient of ammonia is 2. If the sentence does not come, read Step 4 again — that sentence is the lesson.

    Retrieval check 1 of 2 · notes closed

    Write the four steps of the detour from memory, naming the unit each line ends in.

    Model answer. 1 — Balance the equation and read the coefficients as molecules, then as moles. 2 — n(given) = m ÷ M, answer in mol. 3 — n(wanted) = n(given) × coefficient of wanted ÷ coefficient of given, still in mol. 4 — m(wanted) = n × M of the wanted substance, answer in g.

    In a 5-mark question these are four of the five marks, and the fifth is the balanced equation itself. An answer that jumps from grams to grams scores the equation mark alone.

    Where marks are lost

    Pitfalls

    "The ratio is 2 : 1, so 2 g of this makes 1 g of that." CH-S02
    This is the most common error in the whole unit, and it survives into adulthood. Coefficients count particles; grams are counted by a balance and the two scales are linked only through M. In 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O the particle ratio 2 : 1 corresponds to a mass ratio of 4 g : 32 g, which is 1 : 8. Writing n = m ÷ M on the line before the ratio is the entire defence.
    Why it feels right: the numbers in the equation are the only numbers on the page, the question gives grams, and multiplying the two feels like using what you were given. Nothing in the printed equation warns that its numbers live in a different unit from the data.
    "To balance it, change H₂O into H₂O₂ — now the oxygens match." CH-S05
    Balancing changes the big numbers in front, never the small numbers inside. A subscript is part of the substance's identity: H2O is water and H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, which is a different chemical with a different molar mass. Balanced equations are made by putting coefficients in front until each element's atom count matches on both sides.
    Why it feels right: both numbers are digits sitting next to a formula and both do make the atom counts move, so the eye treats them as the same kind of adjustment. The distinction is chemical, not arithmetical, so arithmetic alone never catches it.
    "1 mol of the reactant gives 1 mol of the product, near enough." CH-S01
    Only when the two coefficients happen to be equal. In N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3, one mole of nitrogen gives two moles of ammonia, and one mole of hydrogen gives two-thirds of a mole of it. The ratio line is written out even when it looks like 1 : 1, because writing it is what makes the examiner's mark available and what catches the case where it is not.
    Why it feels right: the first equations anyone meets — CaCO3 → CaO + CO2, Na + Cl reactions written casually — really are 1 : 1, so the habit forms on a run of examples where skipping the ratio never costs anything.
    "The particle picture is just decoration — the formula is the real chemistry." CH-S06
    The picture is the reason the formula works. Two molecules pairing with one is a fact about the flask; n = m ÷ M is only the translation between that fact and the balance reading. Students who can run the arithmetic but cannot draw the flask are the ones who cannot tell whether an answer is absurd — and an absurd answer left on the page costs every mark below it.
    Why it feels right: the exam prints formulas, not pictures, so the picture looks like something for younger classes. It is doing the checking work that nothing else on the page does.

    Retrieval check 2 of 2 · discriminate the pitfall

    The question where grams sneak into the ratio

    Sodium burns in chlorine: 2Na + Cl2 → 2NaCl. What mass of sodium chloride is made when 46 g of sodium reacts completely? (Na = 23, Cl = 35.5 g/mol.)

    Now use it: without writing any working, say what mass of chlorine gas was consumed. (71 g — 1 mol of Cl₂.)

    Produce the answer · nothing is multiple choice from here

    Three problems, scaffolding removed one rung at a time

    Rung 1 (fully worked) was the table above. These are rungs 2, 3 and 4.

    Rung 2 · Completion — the start is done for you

    Limestone decomposes on strong heating: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. What mass of carbon dioxide is released from 50 g of calcium carbonate? (Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16 g/mol.)

    M(CaCO3) = 100 g/mol  ·  n(CaCO3) = 50 g ÷ 100 g/mol = 0.5 mol

    Two lines are left: cross with the mole ratio, then return to grams.

    1. 1Ratio CaCO₃ : CO₂ is 1 : 1, so n(CO₂) = 0.5 mol × 1 ÷ 1 = 0.5 molThe ratio line is written even when it is 1 : 1 — that is where the mark is.
    2. 1M(CO₂) = 12 + 2(16) = 44 g/molThe product's own molar mass, not the limestone's.
    3. 1m(CO₂) = 0.5 mol × 44 g/mol = 22 gmol × g/mol = g. Sanity check: 22 g of gas from 50 g of solid leaves 28 g of CaO.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 3 · Faded — one nudge, then it is yours

    Hydrogen burns in oxygen: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. What mass of water is formed when 8.0 g of hydrogen burns in excess oxygen? (H = 1, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Nudge: M(H₂) is 2 g/mol, not 1 g/mol — hydrogen gas travels in pairs.

    1. 1M(H₂) = 2(1) = 2 g/molThe subscript 2 is part of the substance, so it is part of its molar mass.
    2. 1n(H₂) = 8.0 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 4.0 molLeave the mass world before doing anything else.
    3. 1Ratio H₂ : H₂O is 2 : 2, so n(H₂O) = 4.0 mol × 2 ÷ 2 = 4.0 molEqual coefficients, so the amounts match — but the line still gets written.
    4. 1M(H₂O) = 18 g/mol; m = 4.0 mol × 18 g/mol = 72 gCheck: 8 g of hydrogen plus 64 g of oxygen makes 72 g of water.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 4 · Cold — exam conditions, no help on the page

    Sodium hydroxide is neutralised by sulphuric acid: 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. 8.0 g of sodium hydroxide reacts completely. Find (a) the mass of sodium sulphate formed and (b) the mass of sulphuric acid used. (Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1, S = 32 g/mol.)

    Worth 5 marks in the real paper. Give yourself 12 minutes — the exam pays about 2.4 minutes per mark.

    1. 1M(NaOH) = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40 g/mol; n(NaOH) = 8.0 g ÷ 40 g/mol = 0.20 molLeave the mass world first, on the given substance.
    2. 1Ratio NaOH : Na₂SO₄ is 2 : 1, so n(Na₂SO₄) = 0.20 × 1 ÷ 2 = 0.10 molWanted on top, given underneath, so mol cancels into mol.
    3. 1M(Na₂SO₄) = 2(23) + 32 + 4(16) = 142 g/mol; m = 0.10 × 142 = 14.2 gPart (a). The product's own molar mass.
    4. 1Ratio NaOH : H₂SO₄ is 2 : 1, so n(H₂SO₄) = 0.10 molA second ratio from the same equation — the given amount is reused, never recalculated.
    5. 1M(H₂SO₄) = 98 g/mol; m = 0.10 mol × 98 g/mol = 9.8 gPart (b). Mass check: 8.0 g + 9.8 g = 14.2 g + 3.6 g of water.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Now the pretest

    What you predicted before the lesson

    Delayed on purpose. Comparing what you thought then with what you know now is the part that sticks — especially anywhere you were confident and wrong.

    P1 · The coefficients count particles and moles, never grams.
    2 mol of H₂ is 4 g because M(H₂) = 2 g/mol, while 1 mol of O₂ is 32 g because M(O₂) = 32 g/mol. The particle ratio 2 : 1 is a mass ratio of 4 g : 32 g. This is misconception CH-S02, the most common error in the unit.

    P2 · 1 mol of N₂ reacts with 3 mol of H₂.
    Coefficients are a ratio of amounts in mol. In grams that same line reads 28 g of nitrogen with 6 g of hydrogen, which looks nothing like 1 : 3 — that is the whole reason the detour through moles exists.

    P3 · The balanced equation, then n = m ÷ M for the calcium carbonate.
    CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, then n = 10 g ÷ 100 g/mol = 0.1 mol. The mole ratio has nothing legal to act on until that amount exists on the page. Compare that with what you wrote before the lesson.

    No score, by design. What matters is which one surprised you — that is the one to bring to Hermes tonight.
