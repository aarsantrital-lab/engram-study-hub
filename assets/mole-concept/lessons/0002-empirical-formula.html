    Empirical Formula from Percentage Composition

    About 20 minutes · PACER P (procedural) · In the NEB paper this shows up as a 4-mark or 5-mark Group B question, and it is the most repeated calculation in the whole Stoichiometry unit

    Before any teaching

    Pretest — 3 questions

    The big idea

    Percentages are masses. Formulas are counts. Moles are the only way across.

    An empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound. A laboratory cannot hand you that ratio directly — it hands you percentages by mass, because a balance weighs, it does not count. Percentages become a ratio of atoms only after every element's mass has been turned into an amount in mol, and the mole was built for exactly this crossing in Lesson 1.

    % by mass → g in 100 g → n = m ÷ (atomic mass) → ÷ smallest → × 2 or × 3

    Think of a dal-bhat recipe: "one cup of rice to two cups of water" is a ratio, and it holds whether you cook for four people or forty. The kitchen scale reading changes; the recipe does not. An empirical formula is that recipe, written in atoms.

    The method

    The ÷smallest method — four lines, every time

    Four lines, in this order, on every percentage-composition question in the paper. Each line is worth a mark, so each line goes on the page even when the arithmetic is easy enough to do in your head.

    The atomic masses this topic uses — read the board before any molar-mass work, because the paper does not always print it:

    ElementHCN ONaS ClCa
    Atomic mass (amu, per atom)1121416233235.540
    Molar mass (g/mol, per mole)1121416233235.540

    The same digits, two units: 12 amu is one carbon atom, 12 g/mol is one mole of carbon atoms. Division in this method uses the g/mol column.

    Step 1 · Percentages become grams
    Assume a 100 g sample, so 40.0% carbon becomes 40.0 g of carbon. The assumption is free because the answer is a ratio — Lesson 1's point that grams are only ever a doorway to moles.
    Step 2 · Grams become moles
    Divide each element's mass by that element's own atomic mass in g/mol. Carbon by 12, hydrogen by 1, oxygen by 16 — never all three by the same number, and never by the molar mass of the whole compound. Write mol on every line.
    Step 3 · Divide by the smallest
    Take the smallest of those mole values and divide every mole value by it. The smallest becomes exactly 1 and the rest become the ratio. Keep two decimal places here — rounding early is what turns 2.01 into 2 safely and 2.50 into a wrong answer.
    Step 4 · Clear the fraction
    If a number ends in .5, multiply every number by 2; if in .33 or .67, by 3. Multiply all of them, never only the awkward one, then write the formula with the whole numbers as subscripts.

    Write the unit on every line. An examiner marks the line, not your intention.

    Now act

    Why it behaved that way

    Moving the sample mass moves both mole values by the same factor, so their ratio never shifts — that is why Step 1 may assume 100 g without asking anyone's permission. Moving the carbon share is a different matter: it changes the two mole values by different factors, and the formula jumps. At 27.3% carbon the ratio is 1 : 2 and the compound is CO2; at 42.9% carbon it is 1 : 1 and the compound is CO. Nothing in between is a gentle blend — a formula is made of whole atoms.

    That is Step 3 doing its job: the ÷smallest line converts two raw mole numbers into the ratio the examiner is asking for.

    Worked example · rung 1 of 4 (fully worked)

    40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O

    A compound of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen is 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16 g/mol.)

    ElementMass in 100 g (g)Atomic mass (g/mol) Amount (mol)÷ smallest
    C40.0123.331.00
    H6.716.702.01
    O53.3163.331.00
    Smallest is 3.33 molEmpirical formula = CH₂O

    Units check: 40.0 g ÷ 12 g/mol = 3.33 mol. The ÷ smallest column is mol ÷ mol, so it is a bare ratio with no unit — and 2.01 rounds to 2 because the gap is measurement noise, not a fraction.

    Self-explanation (do not skip): say out loud why hydrogen ends up with the biggest mole value while having the smallest mass. If the sentence does not come, read Step 2 again — that sentence is the lesson.

    Retrieval check 1 of 2 · notes closed

    Write the four lines of the ÷smallest method from memory, with the unit each line ends in.

    Model answer. 1 — Assume 100 g, so each percentage becomes that many grams. 2 — Amount of each element, n = mass ÷ its own atomic mass, answer in mol. 3 — Divide every mole value by the smallest one; the result is a bare ratio. 4 — Multiply the whole ratio by 2 or 3 to clear a fraction, then write the subscripts.

    Those four lines are the four marks in a 4-mark question. Line 2 without the unit mol is the one examiners most often refuse.

    Where marks are lost

    Pitfalls

    "40% carbon means 40 carbon atoms, so the formula is C₄₀H₆.₇O₅₃.₃." CH-M01
    A percentage by mass is a share of the grams, not a count of atoms. Carbon atoms are 12 times heavier than hydrogen atoms, so 40 g of carbon holds far fewer atoms than 40 g of hydrogen would. The division by atomic mass in Step 2 is precisely the repair — before it, the numbers are masses; after it, they are counts.
    Why it feels right: percentages look like counts everywhere else in school — 40% of a class is 40 students out of 100 — and nothing in the wording of the question warns that these particular hundred units are grams, not atoms.
    "Divide every mass by the molar mass of the compound." CH-M03
    Each element's mass is divided by that element's atomic mass in g/mol: carbon by 12 g/mol, hydrogen by 1 g/mol, oxygen by 16 g/mol. Dividing all three by one number scales them all equally, which leaves the ratio exactly where it started and produces the C₄₀H₆.₇O₅₃.₃ answer wearing a different disguise. The compound's molar mass belongs to the next lesson, not this one.
    Why it feels right: Lesson 1 drilled n = m ÷ M with a single M for the whole substance, and the hand repeats that move before the head notices that here there are three separate substances being counted.
    "6.70 mol of hydrogen means 6.70 hydrogen molecules." CH-M04
    In this method every mole value counts atoms of one element inside the compound, not molecules and not formula units. 6.70 mol of hydrogen atoms is 6.70 × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms, and the number 6.70 becomes the subscript 2 only after the ÷smallest step. Say the words out loud — "moles of hydrogen atoms" — and the subscript stops looking like a molecule count.
    Why it feels right: "mole" was introduced with molecules of water and molecules of carbon dioxide, so the word arrives already attached to whole molecules, and nobody restates it when the counting switches to single atoms.
    "The ratio came out 1 : 2.5, so I round it to 1 : 3." CH-S08
    Rounding 2.5 up invents a compound that was never in the beaker. A ratio may be scaled, so multiply every number by 2 and get the whole-number ratio 2 : 5. Rounding is allowed only for measurement noise — 2.01 or 1.98, never 2.5 or 2.33. And the result is still a ratio: CH₂O is the empirical formula of three different real compounds, which is the problem Lesson 3 solves.
    Why it feels right: every other calculation in the paper ends with a rounded number, so rounding feels like the normal last step rather than the one place where it destroys the answer.

    Rung 2 · Completion — the start is done for you

    A compound is 52.2% carbon, 13.0% hydrogen and 34.8% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16 g/mol.)

    In 100 g: 52.2 g C, 13.0 g H, 34.8 g O  ·  n(C) = 52.2 ÷ 12 = 4.35 mol

    Three lines are left: the other two amounts, the ÷smallest step, the formula.

    1. 1n(H) = 13.0 g ÷ 1 g/mol = 13.0 mol; n(O) = 34.8 g ÷ 16 g/mol = 2.175 molEach element divided by its own atomic mass, unit mol on both.
    2. 1Smallest is 2.175 mol: C = 4.35 ÷ 2.175 = 2.00, H = 13.0 ÷ 2.175 = 5.98, O = 1.00Divide all three by the smallest; the smallest always becomes 1.
    3. 1Empirical formula = C₂H₆O5.98 rounds to 6 — that gap is measurement noise, not a fraction to clear.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Rung 3 · Faded — one nudge, then it is yours

    A white solid is 43.4% sodium, 11.3% carbon and 45.3% oxygen by mass. Find its empirical formula. (Na = 23, C = 12, O = 16 g/mol.)

    Nudge: start with the 100 g assumption, exactly as in the worked table.

    1. 1In 100 g: 43.4 g Na, 11.3 g C, 45.3 g OPercentages become grams under the 100 g assumption.
    2. 1n(Na) = 43.4 ÷ 23 = 1.887 mol; n(C) = 11.3 ÷ 12 = 0.942 mol; n(O) = 45.3 ÷ 16 = 2.831 molSodium by 23, carbon by 12, oxygen by 16 — its own atomic mass each time.
    3. 1Smallest is 0.942 mol: Na = 2.00, C = 1.00, O = 3.00Divide all three by 0.942; carbon becomes the 1.
    4. 1Empirical formula = Na₂CO₃Whole numbers already, so no ×2 or ×3 step is needed here.

    Rung 4 · Cold — exam conditions, no help on the page

    A 4.00 g sample of a compound of calcium and chlorine contains 1.44 g of calcium, the rest being chlorine. Find its empirical formula. (Ca = 40, Cl = 35.5 g/mol.)

    Worth 5 marks in the real paper. Give yourself 12 minutes — the exam pays about 2.4 minutes per mark.

    1. 1Mass of chlorine = 4.00 g − 1.44 g = 2.56 gThe data is already in grams, so no 100 g assumption is needed — the ratio does not care.
    2. 1n(Ca) = 1.44 g ÷ 40 g/mol = 0.036 molCalcium divided by its own atomic mass, answer in mol.
    3. 1n(Cl) = 2.56 g ÷ 35.5 g/mol = 0.0721 molChlorine is 35.5 g/mol, a decimal that stays a decimal.
    4. 1Smallest is 0.036 mol: Ca = 1.00, Cl = 2.000.0721 ÷ 0.036 = 2.00, so the ratio is 1 : 2.
    5. 1Empirical formula = CaCl₂Final formula on its own line. That is the line the examiner ticks.
    How many of those lines did you actually write?

    Now the pretest

    What you predicted before the lesson

    Delayed on purpose. Comparing what you thought then with what you know now is the part that sticks — especially anywhere you were confident and wrong.

    P1 · Neither — both land on exactly the same formula.
    Ten times the sample holds ten times as many atoms of every element, so every mole value grows by ten and the ratio between them is untouched. That invariance is the licence for Step 1's 100 g assumption.

    P2 · Hydrogen atoms are far lighter, so many of them still weigh little.
    One carbon atom is 12 amu against hydrogen's 1 amu. In a compound that is 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen, 100 g holds 7.14 mol of carbon atoms and 14.3 mol of hydrogen atoms — twice as many hydrogen atoms carrying a seventh of the mass. Mass share and atom count are different questions.

    P3 · Multiply every number in the ratio by 2, giving 2 : 5.
    A ratio may be scaled but never rounded. 1 : 2.5 scaled by 2 is 2 : 5, which is whole and still the same ratio. Rounding 2.5 up to 3 would describe a different compound. Compare that with what you wrote before the lesson.

    No score, by design. What matters is which one surprised you — that is the one to bring to Hermes tonight.
